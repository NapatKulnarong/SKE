> A good research talk is not a paper read aloud — it’s a **guided tour of your thinking**.

---

## 1. Why it matters

Slide quote (attributed to Einstein): if you can’t explain it simply, you don’t understand it well enough. Research has no impact until someone else can follow it.

| Reason | Point |
| --- | --- |
| **Impact** | Great work gets ignored if the talk is bad |
| **Network** | Clear talks attract collaborators, mentors, funding |
| **Career** | Conferences and interviews both grade this |
| **Thinking** | Preparing the talk exposes weak logic |

**Usual failure modes:** unexplained jargon; dumping every result; nerves (pacing/clarity); no beginning–middle–end; slides that are walls of text or unreadable charts.

## 2. Audience

> If you don’t know who you’re talking to, you’re probably not reaching them.

Ask: what do they **already** know, what do they **need** to get *this* message, what do they **care** about?

| Audience | They want | You do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Field experts** | Depth, precision, novelty | Methods, rigor |
| **General academics** | Big-picture relevance | Define acronyms; simplify |
| **Industry** | ROI, applications, scale | Results and use, not derivations |
| **Public** | Stories, real-world | Analogies, visuals, no jargon |

Depth and tone move with the room: academic = formal/structured; industry = concise/benefits; public = story. Replace formulas with intuition unless the formula *is* the contribution.

**Bayesian inference (from the deck):** do **not** open with \(P(\theta \mid D,I) \propto P(D \mid \theta,I)\,P(\theta \mid I)\). Say: updating your opinion with new evidence — like changing weekend plans when the forecast changes.

## 3. Structure (timed)

Times below are the deck’s budget for a typical talk. Keep people oriented, manage transitions, and make complexity digestible.

| Part | Time | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Intro** | 2–3 min | Hook; research question; roadmap; *so what?* |
| **Background** | 3–5 min | Real problem; gap. Not a literature dump. |
| **Methods** | 5–7 min | What you did; diagrams; innovation only to the depth they need |
| **Results** | 5–7 min | Key findings; graphs not number tables; vs baseline; “expected X, found Y” |
| **Discussion** | 3–5 min | Meaning, implications, limits. Honesty builds credit. |
| **Close** | 2–3 min | Restate the message; contribution; next questions |
| **Q&A** | 2–5 min | Budget it; say so; backup slides |

Intro example from the deck: *How can we predict earthquake damage from satellite images? This work maps structural risk from space with deep learning.*

**Flow:** tell them what you’ll say → say it → remind them. Repeat the core message on purpose.

## 4. Slides

Slides **support** the talk; they do not replace it. Good ones emphasize, guide, and stick. Bad ones overload and look unserious.

| Do | Don’t |
| --- | --- |
| Keywords, figures, diagrams | Paste the paper; read paragraphs |
| Speak the detail; slide = anchor | Full bullet novels on every slide |

**Type:** min 24 pt, ideally 28–32. Title 32–40; body 28–32; labels ≥24. Bold/color to emphasize, not underline. White space.

**Charts:** one idea per figure; label axes, units, terms; no 3D/chartjunk; consistent color, high contrast. Bad = six overlapping matplotlib lines. Good = one series, takeaway highlighted.

**Color:** 2–3 primaries; avoid red/green (colorblind); dark-on-light or reverse. Tools named: ColorBrewer, Coolors.

**Animation:** fade, not bounce. Use it only to build one idea (reveal a bullet, highlight a region).

**Results on screen:** before/after; boxes/arrows; annotate surprises; zoom. Example layout: prediction heatmap | ground truth, mark align vs diverge. Label expected vs observed, CIs, significance.

## 5. Delivery and Q&A

Great research still fails if nobody cares. Talk like a curious friend.

**Voice:** vary tone (not monotone), slow on the point / faster on transitions, project without shouting, **pause** instead of “um/like.”

**Eyes / body:** scan the room; plant feet; open gestures (e.g. three fingers for three reasons); no pen-click, pacing, crossed arms.

**Nerves** (everyone has them): box breathing 4–4–4–4; ground (3 see / 2 hear / 1 feel); rehearse **out loud, standing, with slides**; frame as sharing, not a trial. Confidence from prep, not personality.

Q&A is how you connect and show you own the work — not a trap.

1. **Repeat the question** — room hears it, you get think-time, you can reframe. *“You’re asking whether this scales to larger datasets…”*
2. **Answer short** — one point; optional “three reasons…”; tie back to the research question; no tangents.
3. **Don’t know** — say so: dig into data later / haven’t looked / follow up after. Have a contact/follow-up slide.
4. **Hostile / critique-shaped** — stay professional; don’t get defensive or rush. *“Valid concern — here’s the decision logic.”*
5. **Prep** a list:

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Clarification | How is this different from X? |
| Limitation | Weaknesses of the method? |
| Scope | Real-time systems? |
| Assumptions | Why this model? |

## Takeaways

- Talk = guided tour, not the paper out loud.
- Audience decides depth: formula vs weather-forecast analogy.
- Timed arc: hook → gap → method → story of results → so what → Q&A.
- Slides are visual anchors; voice, pause, and honest Q&A carry the rest.
