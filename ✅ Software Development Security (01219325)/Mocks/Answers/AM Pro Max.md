## MCQ rationales

1. **C — Transitive trust.** A inherits an indirect trusted path through B to C.
2. **C — Audit trail.** Auditing preserves evidence of actions; it does not assign responsibility.
3. **D — Accountability.** Accountability identifies who is answerable for a decision or action.
4. **A — Non-repudiation.** A valid signature makes later denial of the signed approval difficult.
5. **C — Credible scenario.** A confirmed vulnerability needs an abuse or failure scenario before contextual significance can be assessed.
6. **D — Bug to impact.** The development chain is bug → vulnerability → exploit → impact.
7. **A — The Act prevails.** A subordinate ministerial regulation cannot override a higher Act.
8. **C — Criminal distinction.** Criminal law emphasizes punishment and literal reading; civil law emphasizes judgments and intention.
9. **B — Interception.** Secret capture of data in transit is wiretapping or eavesdropping.
10. **C — Damage and disruption.** Malware, altered data, and interrupted service belong to this group.
11. **D — Source concealment.** The course explicitly includes concealing the origin of data or email.
12. **A — NCSC.** Thailand's Cybersecurity Act created the national cybersecurity body and emergency powers.
13. **B — Electricity network.** Energy and utilities are expressly listed critical infrastructure sectors.
14. **C — Patent.** Patents protect inventions, although software patents are uncommon.
15. **C — Trade secret.** The course states trade-secret protection covers espionage but not clean-room reverse engineering.
16. **B — Product logo.** Trademark protects brand identifiers such as names and logos.
17. **D — Share alike.** Adapted content must be shared under compatible alike terms.
18. **C — APPI.** The summary contrasts APPI with GDPR on pseudonymization and mandatory DPOs.
19. **C — Portability.** Portability requires a usable, machine-readable transfer format.
20. **D — Objection.** Objection can require stopping a processing purpose without deleting the account.
21. **D — Automated decisions.** Significant purely automated decisions require a way to contest or obtain recourse.
22. **A — Remedy process.** Complaint, judicial remedy, and compensation need assigned owners and deadline handling.
23. **B — Collector.** Collector describes obtaining data; it is functional rather than a formal GDPR role.
24. **C — Recipient.** A recipient receives data disclosed by another party.
25. **A — Children's health records.** Sensitive health data combined with minors is the strictest case presented.
26. **B — Dark pattern.** Manipulative interface design undermines freely given consent and withdrawal.
27. **A — Targeted filtering.** The analyst narrows attributes to identify one known target.
28. **A — Small-cell inference.** A tiny aggregate can expose facts without publishing rows.
29. **C — MASVS.** MASVS is OWASP's mobile application verification standard.
30. **D — Awareness.** Top 10 highlights common risks; it is not a complete requirement or risk framework.
31. **D — Level 1.** Level 1 is low assurance and can be tested black-box without source.
32. **A — Level 2.** Level 2 is the recommended default and uses coding, architecture, SAST or hybrid, and tests.
33. **C — Level 3.** Level 3 applies Level 2-style evidence at the highest rigor for high-value systems.
34. **C — PCI DSS.** PCI DSS directly governs payment-card security.
35. **D — SD3+C.** The phrase expands to Secure by Design, Default, Deployment and Communication.
36. **D — OWASP mapping.** SAMM, ASVS, and threat modeling map to ISO secure-development concerns.
37. **C — Select, read, choose.** Controls should follow actual applicability and risk, not compliance theater.
38. **B — Requirements Review.** This gate validates security requirements before design proceeds.
39. **B — Design Review.** Threat models and proposed controls are challenged at the secure design gate.
40. **D — Code and scans.** Implementation produces secure code and scanning evidence for code review.
41. **B — Avoided rework.** Early design corrections prevent costly deployed-data, architecture, and migration changes.
42. **C — Convenience and surface.** Each integration adds useful capability and another asset, interface, or credential to protect.
43. **B — Likelihood.** Popular stacks and opportunistic attacker activity increase how likely exploitation is.
44. **B — Exposure.** SLA loss is a consequence driver, not a probability driver.
45. **A — Organizational risk.** Cryptography cannot assign legal duties or ownership between parties.
46. **B — Data flow.** A flow represents information moving between actors, processes, and stores.
47. **C — CWE-501 concern.** Unvalidated input is mixed into a structure treated as trusted.
48. **A — System model.** “What are we working on?” drives decomposition and boundary identification.
49. **B — Verification.** Evidence and residual risk answer whether the work was good enough.
50. **C — Preserve coverage.** Premature feasibility filtering can erase credible threats before prioritization.
51. **D — Fail-safe defaults.** Access not explicitly permitted is denied.
52. **A — Least common mechanism.** Separate workspaces remove a shared information path.
53. **A — Minimize attack surface.** Removing an unnecessary interface removes an opportunity for abuse.
54. **B — Abstraction.** Grouping users into role classes simplifies permission management.
55. **D — Data hiding.** Hiding internal state behind an interface differs from relying on a secret design.
56. **C — DRY.** One authoritative validation rule prevents inconsistent duplicated fixes.
57. **B — YAGNI.** Unneeded features add complexity and attack surface without current value.
58. **D — Weakest link.** Attackers choose the cheapest route, here the shared default credential.
59. **A — Compromise planning.** Secrets require rotation, revocation, detection, and recovery plans.
60. **D — Psychological acceptability.** Unworkable controls prompt users to create insecure workarounds.
61. **B — Transaction authorization.** The OTP confirms a specific high-risk action, not merely login identity.
62. **D — Information flow.** This model formalizes which information flows are authorized.
63. **D — Capability list.** A matrix row gives the objects and rights available to one subject.
64. **B — ACL.** A matrix column gives subjects and rights for one object.
65. **A — Take.** X can pull Y's read right over Z through the take relation.
66. **B — Grant.** Grant pushes a held right outward to another subject.
67. **D — Simple Security.** Bell–LaPadula's simple property forbids reading up.
68. **A — Star property.** The star or confinement property forbids writing secrets down.
69. **D — Access matrix.** Bell–LaPadula's discretionary property uses DAC represented by a matrix.
70. **A — No read down.** Biba blocks high-integrity subjects from ingesting lower-integrity data.
71. **C — No write up.** A low-integrity subject may not modify a higher-integrity object.
72. **A — Consistency.** Biba includes protection of internal and external consistency among its goals.
73. **B — Induction.** A secure start plus security-preserving legal transitions covers every reachable state.
74. **B — Enforcement components.** The TCB is precisely what must be trusted to enforce the policy.
75. **C — Availability scope.** The summary identifies TCB and state machine as models that can touch availability.
76. **D — Authority first.** Authority level establishes the bounds used for confinement and resulting isolation.
77. **A — Isolation.** Restricting locations and resources prevents contact with other applications' resources.
78. **A — Operating-environment isolation.** Virtualization separates OS environments and supports safer testing.
79. **C — Hardware-backed keys.** A TPM stores keys in a mainboard cryptoprocessor.
80. **C — Constrained interface.** Visible or enabled functions vary according to privilege.
81. **B — Allowlist.** Defining valid type, range, length, and format is safer than enumerating bad inputs.
82. **D — Client bypass.** The requester controls the client, so the server must enforce trust.
83. **B — Timeout and logout.** Both shorten the period in which an unattended or stolen session remains usable.
84. **C — Session token.** Logs must not become a secondary store of reusable secrets.
85. **B — Trusted library.** Maintained vetted cryptography is safer than custom or outdated hashing.
86. **B — Patch plan.** Inventory finds what exists; a plan governs updates and response.
87. **A — ROT13.** It is Caesar shift 13 and applying it twice restores the text.
88. **A — Variable shifts.** Vigenère uses a repeating keyword to create polyalphabetic substitution.
89. **D — Processing form.** Block ciphers handle fixed-size units; stream ciphers process continuous streams.
90. **A — Legacy set.** DES is broken, 3DES is disallowed, and RC4 is legacy.
91. **D — Shared-key blast radius.** One leaked key can expose all ciphertext protected by that key.
92. **B — Checksum.** It detects change without binding the result to an author's identity.
93. **B — Different goals.** Encryption supplies confidentiality; signing supplies authenticity and integrity.
94. **A — Purposeful.** Root trust should be valid, authentic, trustworthy, and purposeful.
95. **C — After revocation.** A signature produced after the revocation date is untrusted under the course rule.
96. **C — Future caveat.** RSA faces Shor-based risk, but present quantum systems are not a universal immediate break.
97. **D — Build checkpoint.** SAST and SCA should run before code leaves the lifecycle and feed QA readiness.
98. **A — DAST.** DAST observes runtime behavior and can operate repeatedly alongside incident response.
99. **D — Limited evidence.** A pen test discovers paths sampled by skilled testers but cannot prove none remain.
100. **A — Monitoring.** Continuous production observation catches post-release behavior and incidents.

## Part II — True/False answers

1. **TF1 — True.** Transitive trust creates an indirect path that can bypass a direct restriction.
2. **TF2 — False.** Auditing records what occurred; accountability identifies who must answer for it.
3. **TF3 — False.** Higher Thai law overrides lower law, so a regulation cannot override an Act.
4. **TF4 — True.** Trade-secret protection targets misuse of confidential information, not independent clean-room recreation.
5. **TF5 — True.** GDPR rights include recourse concerning significant purely automated decisions.
6. **TF6 — False.** The summary says APPI does not define pseudonymization and does not require a DPO.
7. **TF7 — False.** Small aggregates can disclose sensitive facts through small-cell inference.
8. **TF8 — True.** Top 10 is awareness guidance, not a substitute for ASVS or contextual risk work.
9. **TF9 — True.** Level 1 is black-box; Levels 2 and 3 add implementation evidence with Level 3 at highest rigor.
10. **TF10 — True.** The savings come from avoiding late redesign, migration, and production remediation.
11. **TF11 — True.** Generation should precede filtering so feasibility bias does not reduce threat coverage.
12. **TF12 — True.** Open design exposes the design to scrutiny while data hiding limits unnecessary internal-state exposure.
13. **TF13 — False.** Bell–LaPadula's simple property forbids reading up regardless of write behavior.
14. **TF14 — True.** Biba prevents low-integrity subjects from writing into higher-integrity objects.
15. **TF15 — False.** Every legal transition must preserve security after the secure initial state.
16. **TF16 — True.** Confinement applies resource restrictions; isolation is the separation those restrictions produce.
17. **TF17 — False.** Fault tolerance uses redundancy; failure management covers exception handling and input validation.
18. **TF18 — True.** Client checks aid usability, but only server-side checks are under trusted enforcement.
19. **TF19 — True.** The course treats signatures after revocation as untrusted, preserving the importance of timing.
20. **TF20 — False.** A pen test can reveal attack paths but cannot prove the absence of every other path.
