## MCQ Rationales

1. **B — Integrity.** The data was altered incorrectly; continued service and secrecy do not preserve correctness.
2. **B — Subject.** Roles are assigned per request, so the service becomes the requester when it asks the key service.
3. **A — Authorization.** Valid credentials prove identity, but a separate decision must limit which region the employee may export.
4. **C — Assess in context.** A finding is evidence; reachability, affected assets, probability, and exposure determine risk.
5. **D — Residual risk.** This is the risk that remains after controls are applied.
6. **A — Both may apply.** PDPA follows presence in Thailand, while GDPR can follow EU data subjects worldwide.
7. **D — Permission is needed.** Copyright is automatic, and public availability does not create a licence.
8. **C — LGPL permits this use.** Using the library need not relicense the whole application; a fork of the library remains LGPL.
9. **A — System and data access differ.** Being allowed into a system does not authorize access to every object inside it.
10. **D — Hosting creates concern.** The course notes possible liability for platforms that allow prohibited user content.
11. **C — Erasure.** Copies spread across tables and derived stores make complete deletion a schema-level problem.
12. **B — Consent is specific.** Insurance pricing is a new purpose and cannot be justified by broadening the original consent afterward.
13. **D — Processor.** It acts on instructions; the organization deciding why and how is the controller.
14. **A — Pseudonymized.** The mapping still exists, so reidentification remains possible and the data remains regulated.
15. **C — Linkage.** Combined quasi-identifiers can identify people even after direct identifiers are removed.
16. **B — Small-cell suppression.** Withholding groups below a threshold directly reduces inference from tiny populations.
17. **A — Generalization.** Regional values retain some utility while reducing uniqueness and analytical precision.
18. **D — ISO/IEC 27001.** It contains certifiable ISMS requirements; 27002 and 800-53 are control catalogs.
19. **B — ASVS.** It provides tiered app requirements, verification guidance, metrics, and a procurement basis.
20. **C — SSDF.** NIST SP 800-218 is designed to integrate secure practices into an existing SDLC.
21. **D — Requirements/design.** Opposing data needs are a design conflict; delaying the decision causes expensive redesign.
22. **D — Feed back.** The SSDLC loops because later evidence can require revision of an earlier model or design.
23. **C — Enumerate, then prioritize.** Threat modeling finds scenarios; risk assessment ranks their significance.
24. **A — Critical-service harm.** Consequences to emergency service and life drive exposure even when probability is low.
25. **B — Avoid.** Removing the unnecessary activity eliminates the associated risk source.
26. **C — Accept.** Acceptance is valid when contextual harm is low and the decision is documented.
27. **A — Transfer.** Insurance shifts defined financial loss to another party.
28. **B — Bound.** The cap limits blast radius where prevention cannot be guaranteed.
29. **C — Validate explicitly.** Crossing a trust boundary does not create trust; checking the data does.
30. **D — Structured coverage.** STRIDE is a systematic prompt, not a ranking method or the entire threat-modeling process.
31. **A — Tampering.** Unauthorized alteration breaks integrity.
32. **C — Repudiation.** Missing evidence lets the actor deny an action and weakens accountability.
33. **B — Elevation of privilege.** The employee reaches a function beyond assigned authorization.
34. **D — DoS and availability.** Flooding prevents legitimate use of the service.
35. **A — Information disclosure.** The endpoint exposes confidential data to unauthorized viewers.
36. **B — Include failures.** Credible threats include mistakes, insiders, and system failures without malicious actors.
37. **D — Principal and object.** It names the caller–document relationship and has a clear deny outcome.
38. **C — Ownership is missing.** Authentication establishes the principal but not permission for the requested object.
39. **B — Specifications and tests.** Threat modeling informs design controls and the plan used to verify them.
40. **A — Positive requirement.** It states specific expected system behavior that can pass or fail a test.
41. **C — Least privilege.** The account should hold only rights needed for its current deployment task.
42. **D — Separation of privilege.** Access depends on multiple independent conditions rather than one; the token is not a second person, so separation of duties does not fit.
43. **D — Separation of duties.** One person should not control a critical process from initiation through execution.
44. **B — Complete mediation.** Every access to every object needs a current check, not only the login event.
45. **A — Verify every request.** Zero trust does not grant generic trust merely because traffic is internal.
46. **A — Least power.** A narrow parser has less capability to abuse than a general evaluator.
47. **C — Economy of mechanism.** Smaller mechanisms and simpler interfaces are easier to analyze and test.
48. **B — Open design.** Security should rely on secret keys, not secrecy of the protocol design.
49. **A — Avoid unnecessary information.** The generic message reduces user enumeration while preserving usable feedback.
50. **C — Strategic friction.** A proportional speed bump helps establish intent before an irreversible action.
51. **B — Secure defaults.** The shipped configuration starts safe and requires a deliberate act to weaken it.
52. **D — Fail-closed.** When authorization cannot be established, the system denies access.
53. **C — Physical fail-safe.** Opening protects people, unlike a digital fail-closed response that protects data.
54. **A — Fail-soft.** The larger system keeps operating after one component fails.
55. **B — Independent layers.** Validation, authorization, and bounding address different failure paths rather than repeating one check.
56. **C — Bell–LaPadula.** Its no-read-up/no-write-down rules protect classified confidentiality.
57. **D — Biba.** Its no-read-down/no-write-up rules prevent lower-integrity data from contaminating higher-integrity objects.
58. **A — Clark–Wilson.** Subjects use trusted programs and well-formed transactions rather than modifying objects directly.
59. **D — Brewer–Nash.** Access changes based on prior viewing within a conflict-of-interest class.
60. **B — Take-Grant.** It explicitly models taking, granting, creating, and removing rights.
61. **A — ACL column.** The object-centric column shows which subjects can access that object.
62. **C — Concept versus implementation.** The reference monitor checks every access; the security kernel is the trusted implementation.
63. **B — State machine.** Secure initialization plus security-preserving legal transitions gives the induction argument.
64. **B — Assurance.** Existence creates trust; recurring evidence about reliability creates and maintains assurance.
65. **C — Confinement.** Sandboxing restricts a process to named resources and yields isolation.
66. **A — Memory protection.** It enforces per-process memory bounds regardless of the applications running.
67. **D — Fault tolerance.** Redundancy removes single points of failure and allows continued operation.
68. **D — Parameterized queries.** They preserve the separation between input data and SQL commands.
69. **A — Server-side authorization.** UI hiding changes visibility, not permission at the trusted enforcement point.
70. **C — Hash and salt.** Password verification should be one-way; the original password need not be recoverable.
71. **D — Regenerate after login.** Rotation prevents a pre-authentication identifier from remaining valid in the authenticated session.
72. **B — Generic outside, detail inside.** Users should not receive secrets, while protected logs retain diagnostic value.
73. **A — Full-stack inheritance.** Libraries, frameworks, runtimes, servers, and configuration all contribute risk.
74. **C — It bounds quantity.** `LIMIT 1` cannot prove that the selected row is the intended object.
75. **D — Abort on invariant failure.** Zero or multiple matches disprove the assumption required for safe deletion.
76. **A — Scope plus independent approval.** Authorization alone does not establish current intent for a high-blast-radius action.
77. **C — AES.** Symmetric encryption is efficient for bulk data when the same service controls storage and restoration.
78. **B — Diffie–Hellman.** It lets both parties derive a common secret without sending that secret.
79. **C — Complementary strengths.** Asymmetric crypto solves identity/key distribution; symmetric crypto supplies payload speed.
80. **D — ECC.** It offers comparable strength with much smaller keys, fitting constrained devices.
81. **B — Authenticity and integrity.** Signing does not hide readable content or guarantee service availability.
82. **A — Identity binding.** A signature proves possession of a private key, not who owns that key.
83. **D — Revoke.** Revocation tells relying parties to stop trusting the compromised certificate.
84. **C — Chain of trust.** The subordinate is trusted because a pre-trusted root signs or vouches for it.
85. **A — Hash/checksum.** Change detection does not require identity binding or confidentiality.
86. **D — SAST.** It examines source for suspicious patterns before execution.
87. **B — SCA.** It inventories components and checks them for known dependency risks.
88. **B — DAST.** It observes behavior at the interface of a running application.
89. **C — Penetration testing.** Human adversarial work is suited to combining weaknesses into attack paths.
90. **A — Review and targeted tests.** These mechanisms know the intended ownership requirement and can directly evaluate it.
91. **C — Contextualize.** Sensitive child-health data can make a nominally medium finding the highest business risk.
92. **B — RBAC.** Stable permissions aligned to job function are simpler as roles.
93. **D — ABAC.** Multiple contextual properties can express decisions that static roles cannot.
94. **A — Split the systems.** Separate pipelines reconcile anonymous analysis with controlled, consented intervention.
95. **C — Control catalogs.** ISO 27002 and NIST SP 800-53 offer controls rather than ISMS certification.
96. **D — Level 3.** It is intended for high-value transactions and the highest assurance rigor.
97. **A — Effective restriction.** A ticket is not enough; the system must genuinely stop processing.
98. **B — Rectification.** Corrected data must become the value used by later processing and decisions.
99. **B — k-anonymity.** It requires each record to be indistinguishable from at least `k−1` others on selected attributes.
100. **D — Reassess residual risk.** Controls change probability or exposure; the remaining risk must be evaluated and recorded.

## Part II Model Answers and Rubrics

### 1. Object ownership after authentication

- **1 point:** Identifies broken object-level authorization, not authentication.
- **1 point:** Notes that the student is a valid principal but does not own every response object.
- **1 point:** Requirement example: “Every request for a survey response shall verify that the authenticated student owns that response and deny access otherwise.”
- **1 point:** Negative test changes `responseId` to another student's record and expects denial without disclosure.

### 2. CIA and accidental modification

- **1 point:** Identifies **integrity** as the failed property.
- **1 point:** Applies it to unauthorized or incorrect modification of the dosage.
- **1 point:** Explains that a legitimate user may already have confidential access.
- **1 point:** Names a relevant integrity control, such as validation, constrained updates, review, or audit-supported transactions.

### 3. Overlapping privacy regimes

- **1 point:** PDPA may apply because the company processes in Thailand.
- **1 point:** GDPR may apply to EU data subjects even when processing occurs abroad.
- **1 point:** Recognizes that regimes can apply simultaneously.
- **1 point:** Recommends mapping obligations and designing to meet the strictest applicable requirement rather than selecting only one law.

### 4. Repository code and licences

- **1 point:** Copyright is automatic; public code without a licence remains all rights reserved.
- **1 point:** Permission or a valid licence is needed before reuse.
- **1 point:** GPL copyleft can require the whole distributed project to become GPL.
- **1 point:** LGPL generally permits use by a proprietary application, while modifications/forks of the library remain LGPL.

### 5. Linkage and defenses

- **1 point:** Explains joining the released table to an obtainable identity source using quasi-identifiers.
- **1 point:** Shows that a unique match reveals a named person plus diagnosis without breaching the publisher's system.
- **1 point:** Gives one suitable defense, such as coarsening, small-cell suppression, k-anonymity, or avoiding row-level release.
- **1 point:** Gives a second defense and states a cost, such as reduced precision, reduced record-level utility, or loss of a collected field.

### 6. Aggregate analysis and intervention

- **1 point:** Identifies conflicting requirements: anonymity for analysis versus identity for follow-up.
- **1 point:** Treats the conflict as a requirements/design finding rather than “doing both” in one table.
- **1 point:** Proposes a genuinely anonymized aggregate pipeline and a separate intervention pathway.
- **1 point:** Gives the intervention path separate consent, access, retention, and logging so reidentification becomes an authorized act.

### 7. Consent after design

- **1 point:** Consent must be specific, informed, and purpose-bound.
- **1 point:** An unsettled schema/purpose makes the description inaccurate.
- **1 point:** The team must decide data, purposes, recipients, retention, and processing roles first.
- **1 point:** New purposes later require a new lawful basis or consent, not silent policy expansion.

### 8. Pseudonymization versus anonymization

- **1 point:** Pseudonymization replaces identifiers but preserves a mapping.
- **1 point:** It remains reversible personal data and regulated.
- **1 point:** True anonymization destroys the mapping and can leave privacy-law scope.
- **1 point:** Follow-up requires reversibility, so the study must accept regulation or abandon individual follow-up.

### 9. Selecting standards and guidance

- **1 point:** Chooses **ISO/IEC 27001** for a certifiable ISMS.
- **1 point:** Chooses **OWASP ASVS**, normally Level 2 as the default, for app verification requirements.
- **1 point:** Chooses **NIST SP 800-218 SSDF** for integrating secure practices into an existing SDLC.
- **1 point:** Justifies each by its purpose rather than treating the resources as interchangeable.

### 10. SSDLC feedback

- **1 point:** States that evidence from any stage can force revision of earlier decisions.
- **1 point:** Example: a test exposes a missing trust boundary or ownership requirement.
- **1 point:** Sends the issue back to threat model/design/requirements, then updates code and tests.
- **1 point:** Re-verifies and reassesses residual risk before sign-off.

### 11. Modeling through treatment

- **1 point:** Model assets, actors, flows, stores, entry points, and trust boundaries first.
- **1 point:** Threat modeling then enumerates credible failures; STRIDE may structure this step.
- **1 point:** Risk assessment ranks scenarios using probability and exposure in context.
- **1 point:** Treatment decides mitigate, accept, transfer, or avoid; scanner labels alone do not determine it.

### 12. STRIDE on a health-data flow

- **1 point:** **S/T:** authenticate the sender; protect against alteration with authenticated transport, signatures, or validation.
- **1 point:** **R/I:** maintain protected audit evidence; encrypt and authorize access to prevent disclosure.
- **1 point:** **D/E:** rate-limit or add resilience; enforce server-side least privilege so the flow cannot gain rights.
- **1 point:** Clearly covers all six categories and ties each to the mobile-to-API flow.

### 13. Non-malicious threats

- **1 point:** Names a mistaken authorized user, such as accidental deletion or wrong data entry.
- **1 point:** Names system failure, such as expired certificates, failed dependencies, or unavailable components.
- **1 point:** Explains why authentication does not stop either source.
- **1 point:** Gives relevant controls, such as bounded actions/confirmation and monitoring/redundancy/recovery.

### 14. Testable statement access

- **1 point:** Names the principal: the authenticated customer/caller.
- **1 point:** Names the object: the requested account statement.
- **1 point:** States an enforceable rule: verify ownership or explicit permission on every request and deny otherwise.
- **1 point:** Negative test requests another customer's statement and expects denial with no data leakage.

### 15. Prevent, bound, recover

- **1 point:** **Prevent:** server-side authorization, validation, least privilege, and complete mediation.
- **1 point:** **Bound:** cap rows/files, rate-limit exports, segment tenants, and show scope.
- **1 point:** **Recover:** retain protected audit records, revoke sessions/keys, restore or notify through an incident plan.
- **1 point:** Explains that prevention cannot reach zero and residual risk remains.

### 16. Four similar failure/default concepts

- **1 point:** **Secure defaults:** safe shipped configuration, such as remote admin disabled.
- **1 point:** **Fail-safe defaults:** deny access not explicitly permitted.
- **1 point:** **Fail securely:** on error, fail closed, expose a generic message, and leave a safe state.
- **1 point:** **Fail-soft:** continue reduced operation after a component fails.

### 17. Physical fail-safe outcomes

- **1 point:** Physical “safe” means protecting people for the emergency exit, so it opens on failure.
- **1 point:** The exit prioritizes evacuation/life safety even though access becomes possible.
- **1 point:** The vault protects assets, so it locks or remains closed on failure.
- **1 point:** Explains that the protected objective determines the safe state; the word does not prescribe one universal position.

### 18. Commercial transaction integrity

- **1 point:** Selects **Clark–Wilson**.
- **1 point:** Maps subject → trusted program → object to employee → transaction service → payroll data.
- **1 point:** Notes well-formed transactions and blocked direct object access.
- **1 point:** Connects two-person approval to separation of duties and explains why Biba's lattice is less specific.

### 19. TCB request path

- **1 point:** The security perimeter bounds the trusted computing base.
- **1 point:** Requests cross that boundary only through a trusted path.
- **1 point:** The reference monitor is the concept that checks every access.
- **1 point:** The security kernel is the trusted hardware/software implementation that enforces the monitor's decision.

### 20. Trust and assurance

- **1 point:** Trust means the sandbox mechanism is present in the design.
- **1 point:** Assurance is evidence that it reliably enforces isolation.
- **1 point:** Assurance must be maintained, not established once.
- **1 point:** Adds bypass/security tests and re-verification after platform or configuration changes.

### 21. Secure coding review

Award **1 point each** for any four correctly paired corrections:

- Validate server-side with an allowlist because client checks are bypassable.
- Use parameterized queries to keep data separate from commands.
- Enforce admin authorization server-side; hiding UI is not authorization.
- Return generic errors and keep protected diagnostic logs.
- Remove keys from code, rotate exposed keys, and use secret management.

### 22. Safe destructive operation

- **1 point:** Authorization answers permission, not whether this action is currently intended.
- **1 point:** `LIMIT 1` bounds the number affected but cannot establish correct identity.
- **1 point:** Resolve an immutable identifier, verify that exactly one intended object matches, and abort on zero or multiple matches.
- **1 point:** Preview scope, require explicit confirmation or two-person approval, log the action, and provide recovery where possible.

### 23. Hybrid protected connection

- **1 point:** Use a CA-validated certificate/PKI to bind the server public key to its identity.
- **1 point:** Use asymmetric cryptography and/or Diffie–Hellman to establish a session secret over the public channel.
- **1 point:** Use symmetric encryption such as AES for efficient bulk traffic.
- **1 point:** Explains that asymmetric crypto solves identity/key distribution but is slower, while symmetric crypto is fast but needs secure key establishment.

### 24. Password storage

- **1 point:** Store a suitable one-way salted password hash.
- **1 point:** Password verification does not require recovering the original.
- **1 point:** Reversible encryption creates a key whose compromise exposes every password.
- **1 point:** Salt prevents identical passwords from producing the same stored value and frustrates precomputed attacks.

### 25. Signature and PKI

- **1 point:** A signature proves integrity and that the holder of the corresponding private key signed.
- **1 point:** It does not provide confidentiality or independently prove the holder is the named vendor.
- **1 point:** Publishing the key through the same untrusted channel permits key substitution.
- **1 point:** A CA certificate and validated chain bind the key to the verified vendor; revocation handles later compromise.

### 26. RSA, ECC, and payload crypto

- **1 point:** Selects ECC for a constrained, battery-powered device.
- **1 point:** Justifies smaller keys and lower resource cost at comparable strength.
- **1 point:** Uses public-key crypto for identity or session-key establishment.
- **1 point:** Uses symmetric crypto for payloads because it is much faster for bulk data.

### 27. Verification mechanism selection

- **1 point:** Suspicious source patterns → **SAST**; vulnerable libraries → **SCA**.
- **1 point:** Runtime-only behavior → **DAST**.
- **1 point:** Chained attack paths → **penetration testing**.
- **1 point:** Proof of the caller–object ownership requirement → **review and security tests**.

### 28. Finding triage

- **1 point:** Validate the finding and rule out a false positive.
- **1 point:** Determine exploit conditions, reachability, and affected assets.
- **1 point:** Assess probability and exposure, then choose treatment: mitigate, accept, transfer/avoid, escalate, or redesign as appropriate.
- **1 point:** Verify controls and reassess/document residual risk; scanner severity is evidence, not the decision.

### 29. RBAC and ABAC

- **1 point:** RBAC assigns permissions through stable job roles and is simpler for the current design.
- **1 point:** ABAC evaluates attributes of subject, object, action, and context.
- **1 point:** Time, location, sensitivity, and department are naturally expressed as attributes.
- **1 point:** Recommends RBAC now and ABAC or a deliberate hybrid when contextual policy is actually required, avoiding unnecessary complexity.

### 30. Student-health dashboard risks

- **1 point:** Small-cell inference → suppress small cells, coarsen data, or enforce a publication threshold.
- **1 point:** Indefinite identifiable retention → minimize data and set/enforce a specific retention period.
- **1 point:** No controller → assign the role and ownership for rights, purpose, and compliance.
- **1 point:** Vendor without agreement → establish processor duties in a data processing agreement and govern access/transfer.
